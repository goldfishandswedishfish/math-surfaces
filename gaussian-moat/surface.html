Picture the "primes" of the flat plane as stepping stones scattered across an endless sea. Could you walk forever on them with steps of some fixed length, say 10? Or would you always end up stranded, surrounded by a moat too wide to jump? The question was asked in 1962. In September 2026, a proof produced by an OpenAI model settled it: you are always stranded, for every step length. Even the size of your island has a cap that depends only on the step length.

The question

Gaussian integers are grid points a + bi in the plane, and they have their own primes: points that can't be factored further. Pick a step limit D. Starting on any Gaussian prime, hop to other Gaussian primes, never more than D away, never revisiting. Can you go on forever?

Why it matters

On the ordinary number line the answer is easy: there are arbitrarily long stretches with no primes, so a walker always gets stuck. In the plane you can walk around a gap, so gaps alone prove nothing. The problem, from Basil Gordon and Theodore Motzkin and popularized by Erdős, became a classic test of how primes are spread in two dimensions.

Why it was hard

Computers could only check walks from one starting point at a time, and only for small steps. The farthest anyone reached from the origin with steps of 6 was a computational result. A proof has to handle every starting prime, every possible winding path, and every step length at once.

How the proof works, in four movesshow

Plain language is on the left, the paper's own terms on the right.

  1. 1

    Swap the primes for a repeating pattern

    Real primes are messy. So cross out every multiple of a few chosen Gaussian primes, like a sieve. Every real prime survives (except the few you used), and what's left repeats like wallpaper. If walkers get stuck in the wallpaper, they get stuck on the primes too.

    Try it: "Build a sieve that traps every walker", below.
    Sieved set 𝒜(𝒫) = {z : z ≢ 0 mod πp, π̄p, p ∈ 𝒫}
    Periodic under QZ², Q = ∏p
  2. 2

    Repetition caps every island

    An island in repeating wallpaper can't contain two points exactly one period apart, or shifting it by that period would give an overlapping island that is the same island, which is impossible for a finite one. So every island has at most Q² points, wherever you start.

    Periodic reduction (after Gethner–Stark, Vardi)
    #C ≤ max{Q², |E| + (KD−1)|E|Q²}
  3. 3

    Follow a hypothetical endless walk

    Now prove the right sieve exists. Imagine a walk that goes on forever, never revisiting. Sample it at random times and record its remainders after dividing by many primes. Because the walk keeps reaching new places, those remainders must keep getting more unpredictable.

    Geometric sampling of distinct differences
    Joint entropy of residues · coverage transfer
  4. 4

    Dodging costs information it can't afford

    To avoid every crossed-out point, each step must "know" which remainders to dodge. With enough primes, that knowledge adds up without limit. But a single step of bounded length can only carry a bounded amount of information. The walk can't pay, so it can't exist.

    Increment words · ≥ c / log T information per batch
    Block subadditivity (cf. Tao's entropy decrement)

No number attached

The proof shows the island-size cap BD exists for every step length D, but doesn't compute it. The sieve it uses is chosen "sufficiently large" after D is fixed.

Stronger than asked

The old question was whether an endless walk exists. The answer here is a uniform bound: every island, from every starting prime (including primes on the axes), has at most BD points.

How sure is this?

The repository reports a Lean formalization of the uniform bound for every real step length. Lean is a proof checker that verifies each step mechanically. The repo also cautions that results may still contain issues while review continues.

Build a sieve that traps every walker

This is the proof's first move, done by hand. Each button crosses out every multiple of one Gaussian prime. What's left repeats forever, and every real Gaussian prime (circled) survives except the ones you used to sieve. If no endless walk exists in what's left, none exists on the primes either, and every island is at most one period squared in size.

Try step limit 1 and cross out 5 and 13. Then try √2: you'll need the "odd" filter too, which keeps only points where a + b is odd. Every Gaussian prime except 1 + i passes that test.
