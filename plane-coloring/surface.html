Color every point of a flat, endless plane so that any two points exactly 1 apart get different colors. How few colors can you get away with? Since 1950 the answer has been known to lie somewhere from 4 to 7. In 2018 it was pushed to at least 5. In September 2026, a proof produced by an OpenAI model ruled out 5 as well. The answer is now 6 or 7.

The question

Imagine a ruler exactly 1 unit long. Wherever you drop it on your colored plane, its two ends must land on different colors. The fewest colors that make this possible is called the chromatic number of the plane. Edward Nelson asked about it in 1950. It's known as the Hadwiger–Nelson problem.

Why it matters

It's one of the most famous easy-to-state open problems in mathematics, sitting where geometry, combinatorics and set theory meet. Shelah and Soifer showed that for close cousins of this question, the answer can even depend on which axioms of set theory you assume. For 68 years nobody could push the lower bound past 4.

Why it was hard

Earlier progress came from finding a finite set of points that can't be colored, like the 1,581-point graph that ruled out 4 colors. Doing the same for 5 colors would need a far bigger graph, and computer searches never found one. A coloring can also be infinitely chaotic, with no shapes or borders to reason about at all.

How the proof works, in four movesshow

The proof assumes a 5-coloring exists and shows that leads to a contradiction. Plain language is on the left, the paper's own terms on the right.

  1. 1

    Tame any coloring

    A coloring might be pure chaos: no regions, no borders, colors scattered like dust. The proof shows that if any valid 5-coloring exists, a well-behaved one does too, breaking the rule only on a sliver so thin it has zero area. It gets there by averaging the chaotic coloring over every shift and rotation, then proving the unpicturable part of that average can be thrown away.

    Transfer theorem: a proper k-coloring exists ⇔ a weak measurable k-coloring exists
    Amenable averaging · Haar rigidity of rotation-invariant laws (Furstenberg–Zimmer)
  2. 2

    Look around every point

    Stand at a point and look at the circle of radius 1 around you. Zoom in on each direction and list which colors you see there; call that list the palette. Using how waves spread around a circle, the proof shows that in almost every direction the palette holds at most two colors.

    Try it: hover the plane below. The red arcs are where your own color sits 1 away.
    Palettes as weak-* limits of angular samples
    ≤ 2 labels a.e. · Fourier decay of circle measure
  3. 3

    Build the borders

    The proof never assumes the colors meet along neat borders. It constructs connected borders itself, by blurring the colors slightly and tracking where they change. Walking a loop around a special point then passes through a cycle of 3, 4 or 5 colors. Each border also bans both of its colors from a band of points about 1 away from it.

    Disk-average smoothing · cyclic centers
    Connected continua Kj with exclusion region Δ(Kj) = {z : min|z−q| < 1 < max|z−q|}
  4. 4

    Rule out every cycle

    A 5-color cycle forces impossible patterns of colors around the circle. A 4-color cycle breaks a simple odd/even count. A 3-color cycle forces six alternating wedges, which leaves an open patch using only 3 colors. But a small 7-point figure, the Moser spindle, fits in that patch and can't be colored with 3. Every case fails, so no 5-coloring exists.

    Try it: color the spindle at the bottom of the page.
    Angular words (5-cycle) · antipodal parity (4-cycle)
    Six alternating sectors + rational placement of the Moser graph (3-cycle)

What's still open

Isbell's hexagon coloring shows 7 colors always work. The answer is 6 or 7, and nobody yet knows which. The paper notes that a May 2026 claim of exactly 7 rests on a false assumption.

Why "any coloring" matters

Under extra rules the bar was already higher: Falconer proved in 1981 that colorings with measurable regions need 5, and Townsend showed that map-like colorings need 6. The new result needs no such rule. It also implies some finite set of points forces 6 colors, but the proof never names one.

How sure is this?

The repository reports Lean formalizations of both halves: no 5-coloring exists, and the 7-coloring works including its borders. Lean is a proof checker that verifies each step mechanically. The repo also cautions that results may still contain issues while review continues.

The 7-point figure at the end of the proof

The Moser spindle (1961) is two diamonds, each made of two equilateral triangles with sides of length 1, hinged at one corner and turned until their far tips sit exactly 1 apart. Every line drawn is length 1, so its ends must get different colors. Click a point to change its color. Can you do it with three colors?

In the proof's last move, a 3-color cycle forces a patch of the plane that uses only three colors. The paper places all seven spindle points inside that patch with exact coordinates. Since the spindle needs a fourth color, the patch can't exist.
