Mahler's conjecture asks a simple-sounding question: which shapes are the least round? It had been open since 1939. In September 2026, an OpenAI model produced proofs that settle it in every dimension. The answer is the cube and its relatives, and the papers also pin down exactly which shapes tie for last.

The question

Every convex shape has a twin, its polar. A big shape has a small twin, so multiplying their volumes gives a fair score for the shape's form, not its size. The ball scores highest. Mahler guessed the cube scores lowest: 4ⁿ/n! in n dimensions.

Why it matters

This score is one of the basic measurements of a shape. It shows up in counting lattice points (Mahler's original motivation), in the geometry of normed spaces, and in symplectic geometry. Bourgain and Milman (1987) proved the true minimum is within an exponential factor of the guess. Nobody could pin down the exact value.

Why it was hard

Most "find the best shape" proofs smooth a shape step by step toward a single winner. Here there is no single winner: the cube, the octahedron and, from 4D on, many mixtures of the two all tie. Before these papers, only dimensions 2 and 3 and special families of shapes were settled.

  1. 1

    Build the shape out of slabs

    A slab is the region between two parallel walls. Any symmetric shape can be approximated as closely as you like by stacking slabs. Its twin is then the shape whose corners point in the slabs' directions, so each wall of the shape becomes a corner of the twin.

  2. 2

    Build a fair measuring device

    The proof uses one special curve, a lens. Send a point around a circle at steady speed and push it through the map. Its height comes out perfectly evenly spread. An ordinary circle doesn't do this.

    See it: "The trick at the heart of the proof", below.
    Conformal map of the disk onto a lens with uniform boundary height (a rotation of Gross's example)
  3. 3

    Turn geometry into chance

    For each way of choosing n of the slabs, pick random points on the lens's edge and combine them into one point. Ask: how likely is it to fit inside every other slab too? An argument from complex analysis forces these chances to add up to at least 1.

    Mass at an isolated holomorphic zero, by Stokes' theorem
    S = ΣI PI ≥ 1
  4. 4

    Turn chance back into volume

    Because the lens's heights are perfectly even, each chance equals the volume of a small pointed piece (a simplex) sitting inside the twin. The pieces never overlap, so together they're no bigger than the twin. Chain the two facts: 1 ≤ the chances ≤ volume × twin's volume × n!/4ⁿ.

    Real simplex identity
    1 ≤ S = (n!/4ⁿ)∫A|ΣX| dX
      ≤ (n!/4ⁿ)|A||A°|
  5. 5

    Find every tie

    If a shape lands exactly on the floor, every inequality above must be perfectly tight. Tracing that back gives a concrete property: in the shape's own notion of distance, any three balls that pairwise overlap share a common point. A classical theorem says only Hanner polytopes (cube-octahedron mixtures) have it.

    See them: "Up a dimension", below.
    Three-ball intersection property → metric medians → Hansen–Lima classification

Two more routes in the same family

Without symmetry, triangles (simplices) are the minimizers, with floor (n+1)ⁿ⁺¹/(n!)². A separate paper proves this. A third paper reaches the symmetric result through symplectic geometry: K × K° always fits a "rigid" ball whose volume approaches 4ⁿ/n!.

How the model got there

Its reasoning summary shows it trying and setting aside a run of known approaches: induction on slices, entropy, Kuperberg's construction, Nazarov's Bergman kernels. A formula of Lundin then pointed it toward "boundary laws" on a planar curve, which became the lens in move 2.

How sure is this?

The repository reports Lean formalizations of both the symmetric and general theorems. Lean is a proof checker that verifies each step mechanically. The repo also cautions that results may still contain issues while review continues.

Try it: a shape and its twin

Draw a shape with no dents (mathematicians say convex) that looks the same when spun halfway around its center. Now build its twin, called the polar. Every flat edge of your shape becomes a corner of the twin, and every corner becomes an edge. Edges far from the center turn into corners close in, so a big shape has a small twin.

Multiply the shape's area by its twin's area. Mahler himself proved the floor in the flat plane: 8 for symmetric shapes. Drag the corners and try to get under it. Then switch to "Any shape" to see where triangles bottom out.

Up a dimension

The same game works for solids, with volume in place of area. In 3D the cube and its twin, the octahedron, sit exactly on the floor. Shapes that look more like a ball score higher. A ball is its own twin and gets the highest score of all.

The ceiling, where the ball wins, has been known since Blaschke and Santaló. The floor was the hard part. From 4D on, new minimizers appear, built by mixing "stack" and "glue" operations (for example square ⊕₁ square). These are the Hanner polytopes, and the papers show they are the only shapes that tie the cube.

The trick at the heart of the proof

The proof never measures a shape directly. It turns the question into a probability, using one special curve. Spin a point around a circle at a steady speed and push it through the map below. Its height on the other side lands evenly between −1 and 1: every height is equally likely. Switch to "Plain circle" to see why that's unusual. On an ordinary circle, heights pile up near the top and bottom.

Where the heights land

Why this helps: the shape is described by slabs, one for each pair of opposite faces. The proof picks random points on the lens's edge and asks how often they line up into a point that fits inside every slab at once. A complex-analysis argument (Stokes' theorem applied to the lens map) shows those chances add up to at least 1. Because the heights are perfectly even, the same sum can be rewritten as volumes of pieces of the twin, and those total at most |K|·|K°|·n!/4ⁿ. Together: |K|·|K°| ≥ 4ⁿ/n!.

The formula, for the curious

F(z) = (8/π²) · Σj≥0 (−1)j z2j+1 / (2j+1)². On the circle its imaginary part is a triangle wave, which climbs at a constant rate, so uniform angles give uniform heights. The lens's half-width at height 0 is 8G/π² ≈ 0.7424, where G is Catalan's constant (whose irrationality is result 005 in the same collection).
