Some shapes can fill all of space when you're only allowed to slide copies around, with no turning or flipping. Whenever that's possible, can you always do it in a neat, endlessly repeating pattern, like bricks in a wall? This was called the periodic tiling conjecture. In September 2026, a proof produced by an OpenAI model built a single 3D shape that fills space but never in a repeating way. Three dimensions is the lowest where this can happen.

The question

Take a shape made of cubes on a grid. Slide copies of it (no rotating) so they fill space with no gaps and no overlaps. If you can do that at all, can you always do it periodically, so the whole arrangement repeats when you shift by some fixed steps in every direction? Grünbaum–Shephard and Lagarias–Wang conjectured yes.

Why it matters

Repeating patterns are what make tiling questions checkable by computer. If every tile that tiles could do it periodically, a program could always decide whether a given shape tiles. Shapes that only tile non-periodically are where that breaks down, and they connect tiling to the limits of computation. (The 2023 "hat" tile is non-periodic too, but it needs rotations and flips.)

Why it was hard

On a line, any tiling repeats (Newman, 1977). In the plane, every tile that tiles also has a repeating tiling (Bhattacharya, 2016). Greenfeld and Tao disproved the conjecture in 2022, but only in an enormous number of dimensions. Getting all the way down to three needed a much tighter encoding.

How the proof works, in four movesshow

The strategy, following Greenfeld and Tao, is to hide a puzzle inside a tile. The puzzle has solutions, but none that repeat. Plain language is on the left, the paper's own terms on the right.

  1. 1

    A Sudoku that can't repeat

    Fill a tall strip of a grid with digits from 1 to p−1 under one rule: along every straight line through the grid, the digits must follow a simple arithmetic pattern. There is a solution, built from the last nonzero digit of numbers in base p. But no solution can repeat as you go up the strip.

  2. 2

    Turn the rules into tiling puzzles

    Each Sudoku rule becomes a tiling puzzle, and all the puzzles must be solved by the same set of slides at once. The new trick is to keep the extra "bookkeeping" dimension a single loop, rather than several, by using groups of different prime sizes.

    Simultaneous tiling equations in ℤ² × ℤ/Qℤ
    Activation and cycle tests · pairwise coprime cyclic factors
  3. 3

    Merge the puzzles into one tile

    Stacking the puzzles with the help of a fresh prime packs all of them into a single tile. That one tile tiles the space, but every tiling of it secretly solves the Sudoku, so none can repeat.

    Stacking construction → one tile F ⊂ ℤ² × ℤ/Qℤ
  4. 4

    Unroll the loop into real 3D space

    A plane times a loop of Q steps is what you get from 3D space if you wrap the height every Q steps. The proof builds a 3D tile from many cubes plus one "marked" cube. If it had a repeating tiling, so would the looped version, which is impossible. This holds even if you're allowed to slide by fractional amounts.

    Quotient ℤ³ → ℤ² × ℤ/Qℤ, kernel ℤ(0,0,Q)
    Rigid representatives · marked cube · unit-cube thickening Ω = T + [0,1]³

How big is the tile?

Enormous, and the paper never states a size. The Sudoku alone needs a prime p above 200 and a strip p² wide, and every later step multiplies the size. The tile isn't required to be connected either.

What exactly is ruled out

"Fully periodic" means repeating along three independent directions. The tile might still have tilings that repeat in one or two directions. Every tiling fails in at least one direction.

How sure is this?

The repository reports a Lean formalization of both the 3D grid version and the solid-cube version, and that three is the lowest dimension where this can happen. Lean is a proof checker that verifies each step mechanically. The repo also cautions that results may still contain issues while review continues.

The pattern that won't repeat

This is the puzzle hidden inside the 3D tile. Write each number m = 1, 2, 3, … in base p and keep only its last nonzero digit. Each digit gets a color, and the sequence is laid out in rows so you can see its structure. It looks almost repeating: every row starts the same way. But zoom out and the multiples of p carry a slower copy of the same pattern, and inside that an even slower one, forever.

Pick a candidate period M. The page compares every m with m + M and marks the mismatches. Some always appear: take m = M·(p − 1), so m + M = M·p. Multiplying by p just adds a zero, so M·p ends in the same digit as M, but M·(p − 1) ends in the negative of it.

Reading along a line

In the paper's grid, the column index n and row index m combine as d·n + e along a line of slope d. Reading the last nonzero digit along any line gives the same simple shape: d·n + e counted in clock arithmetic mod p, with a twist where d·n + e is a multiple of p. That rule along every line is what forces the non-repeating structure.

ring: d·n+e divisible by p · dot: divisible by p² (any digit allowed)

The copies sit at grid points where x + 2y + 3z is a multiple of 4. The tile's four cubes land on remainders 0, 1, 2 and 3, so every cube of space is covered exactly once. Rules like this are why small tiles always seem to repeat. The counterexample has to defeat every such rule.
