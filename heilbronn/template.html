Scatter n dots in a square and look at every triangle formed by three of them. The smallest one is your score: you want it as big as possible. How big can it be? For 75 years the guess was "about 1/n², give or take tiny factors." In September 2026, a proof produced by an OpenAI model showed that guess is wrong. You can beat 1/n² by a genuine power of n.

The question

Hans Heilbronn asked it around 1950. Put n points in a unit square so that no triangle they form is tiny. Three points in a line make a triangle of area zero, so you need to avoid that, and more. Call the best possible smallest area Δ(n). How fast does Δ(n) shrink as n grows?

Why it matters

It's a test case for a deep question: how evenly can points be spread? The same tension between order and randomness shows up in sampling, numerical integration and the theory of "irregularities of distribution." Klaus Roth, a Fields medalist, worked on it for decades.

Why it was hard

Random points and tidy grids both land near 1/n². In 1982 Komlós, Pintz and Szemerédi squeezed out an extra log n factor, which disproved Heilbronn's original guess of exactly 1/n². But beating 1/n² by a whole power of n seemed out of reach, and experts conjectured it was impossible.

How the proof works, in four movesshow

The paper builds a point set, then proves its smallest triangle is at least n−2+η for one fixed η > 0. Plain language is on the left, the paper's own terms on the right.

  1. 1

    Start from a curve with no three dots in a line

    Erdős's old trick: on a grid of whole numbers, take the dots (x, x²) with "clock arithmetic" wrapping. No three are ever in a line, which already guarantees triangles of about 1/n². The new proof gives each dot a random label from a much bigger arithmetic system of this kind.

    See it: "Two classic recipes", below.
    Finite-field parabola (1, ξ, ξ²)ᵀ, ξ ∈ 𝔽rd, d = 41
    Distinct labels ⇒ nonzero determinant
  2. 2

    Write the arithmetic into the digits

    Each dot's coordinates are whole numbers, written in a huge base. The proof hides the "no three in a line" guarantee in one particular digit of the triangle's area. If two dots have different labels, that digit can't be zero, so their triangle can't be tiny.

    Field norm as an alternating polynomial
    Encoded in one coefficient of a base-B determinant (carry control à la Salem–Spencer, Behrend)
  3. 3

    Scramble without changing areas, and block flat triangles

    The dots are mixed by a random transformation that keeps all triangle areas the same, so the guarantee survives while the dots spread out evenly. A second, independent clock-arithmetic condition (points on a curved surface) rules out the remaining danger: three dots exactly in a line.

    Random SL₃ orbits mod h · anisotropic lattice counts
    Affine quadric mod q, Chinese remainder theorem, projective point (u₁/u₃, u₂/u₃)
  4. 4

    Delete the few bad ones

    A tiny triangle can now only appear when labels repeat, and that's rare. So throw in twice as many dots as you need and delete one dot from every bad triangle. Enough survive, and their smallest triangle beats 1/n² by a factor of nη.

    Try it: "Random, then delete", below.
    Alteration (deletion) argument
    Δ(n) ≥ c·n−2+η, η = 2/(45435k + 16)

How big is the improvement?

Astonishingly small. The paper's explicit η uses k = T² + 1, where T is itself a 116-digit number. That puts η around 10−235. The size doesn't matter for the theorem: any fixed power beats every log factor once n is large enough. The authors made no attempt to optimize it.

Earlier attempts

Two earlier preprints claimed bigger improvements. The paper explains where each one breaks: one rests on an unproved assumption, and the other accidentally includes three points in a line.

How sure is this?

The repository reports a Lean formalization of the construction for an infinite sequence of sizes n. That's enough to refute the "almost 1/n²" conjecture. Lean is a proof checker that verifies each step mechanically. The repo also cautions that results may still contain issues while review continues.

The race of exponents

Write the best smallest area as roughly 1/nx. Mathematicians attack x from both sides. Constructions push x down by building good dot sets. Impossibility proofs push x up by showing every dot set has a small triangle. The old conjecture said x is essentially 2. The 2026 result moves the construction mark below 2. The gap is far too thin to see, but it's there.
